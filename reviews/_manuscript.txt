Why Are HPC Monitoring Signals Hard to Forecast?
Intrinsic Predictability Across Eight Telemetry Families of a Tier-0 Supercomputer
Mohsen Seyedkazemi Ardebili
mohsen.seyedkazemi@gmail.com
Working draft — August 6, 2026
Abstract
Operational telemetry from high-performance computing systems resists forecasting to
a degree that is widely reported anecdotally and rarely explained. The usual response is to
increase model capacity. We test the opposite position: that the difficulty is largelyintrinsic
to the signals, differs systematically between signal families, and can be quantified without
fitting any forecaster. Half of that position survives our own test and half does not, and we
report both.
Using the public M100 ExaData release from CINECA’s Marconi100 Tier-0 supercom-
puter, we characterise telemetry from eight heterogeneous plugin families — out-of-band
sensors, operating-system counters, scheduler aggregates, alarm states, facility power, room-
level electrical measurement, cooling-plant control registers and air handlers, and exogenous
weather — collected from the same machine over the same 31 months. (A ninth family, per-
job submission records, is collected but is event-structured rather than a time series, and
is excluded; oi-8.) The families are held fixed in system, workload and period while signal
type varies. For each series we compute model-free predictability measures spanning en-
tropy, periodicity, long memory, non-stationarity, and quantisation, and separately measure
the forecast skill achieved by a tiered suite ranging from naive baselines to gradient-boosted
trees under a strictly causal rolling-origin protocol.
We report four findings. First, the monitoring infrastructure is itself non-stationary: of
573 distinct (plugin, metric) pairs observed across the campaign, only 104 are present in
every month, and those are exactly the out-of-band sensor set. Second, a large share of
what is monitored carries no forecastable information at all — the fraction of series that
never change over a month ranges from 64.8% of alarm-state signals to 8.4% of out-of-band
sensors — so any benchmark that averages over such series reports the composition of its
sample as much as the skill of its models. Third, model capacity does not help: measured
against a same-step naive baseline, median skill by tier at a one-hour horizon runs 0.115
for naive forecasters, -0.064 for statistical ones, -0.327 for neural networks and -0.613 for
gradient-boosted trees, and beyond three hours every tier is worse than repeating the last
value.
Fourth, and contrary to our hypothesis, the predictability measures transfer across signal
families only weakly and inconsistently. Individually they correlate with skill — repetition
fraction at r = +0 .37 (cluster-robust 95% CI [0.26, 0.49] over 261 metric clusters), permu-
tation entropy at−0.334. Jointly, a model trained on every family except one predicts the
held-out family’s skill with a medianR2 of +0.06 across 15 families, positive for 9 of them
but ranging from+0.25 down to−1.88. The mean,−0.16, is pulled below zero by the worst
few families rather than describing the distribution. The failure is localised rather than
general: it is monotone counters, which a drift forecaster predicts almost perfectly for rea-
sons no predictability feature anticipates. What the measures mostly capture is how often
a signal holds still, which is closer to a restatement of forecastability than an explanation of
it.

1 Introduction
Operators of large computing facilities increasingly collect holistic telemetry, and increasingly
want to predict it rather than merely react to it: thermal headroom, power capping, and
proactive maintenance are all forecasting problems. Reported accuracy on this class of signal
is nonetheless poor relative to standard time-series benchmarks, and the literature generally
treats that as a modelling deficiency to be fixed with a stronger model.
We invert the question. Instead of asking which model does best, we ask how much pre-
dictability is present at all, measured independently of any forecaster, and whether that mea-
surement predicts the skill a forecaster subsequently achieves. If it does, “HPC telemetry is
hard to forecast” becomes a quantity with named causes rather than folklore.
Our contributions are: (i) a quantitative census of signal heterogeneity across a 31-month
Tier-0 monitoring campaign, including the instability of the monitoring system itself; (ii) a
model-free predictability profile for each of 1402 series drawn from eight plugin families; (iii)
a strictly causal, scale-free forecast benchmark over four model tiers on the same series; and
(iv) a test of whether the first predicts the second, cross-validated by holding out entire signal
families.
The headline result is negative, and we report it as such: the measures do not transfer
across families. We regard this as the more useful outcome than a confirmation would have
been, because the experiment was constructed so that the hypothesis could fail, and identifying
which part fails narrows what a future predictability theory has to explain.
2 Background
2.1 Marconi100 and the ExaData campaign
Marconi100 was a Tier-0 system at CINECA comprising 980 IBM Power AC922 nodes, each
with two 16-core POWER9 processors and four NVIDIA V100 accelerators. The ExaData
campaign published holistic telemetry for the machine from March 2020 to September 2022,
collected through the ExaMon framework and released as Hive-partitioned Parquet, one archive
per month.
We catalogue every partition of all 31 archives — 10504 partitions holding roughly 55 billion
rows — and extract the subset the study requires. Because node-level series of one metric are
highly redundant, and because nodes sharing a rack, inlet air and workload are not independent
samples (oi-9), analysis runs on a fixed panel of 96primary entities — nodes, cooling devices
or PLC panels — whose membership is a stable hash of the entity label. The hash sees only
the label, so panel membership is identical across every metric and month and cross-metric
comparisons land on the same physical machines. Families keyed by a secondary tag yield
proportionally more series than the cap suggests: Nagios identifies a series by nodeand check
description and reaches 1484 distinct series in a single metric-month, where the 96 bound applies
to the node dimension alone.
2.2 The nine plugin families
The value of this dataset for the present question is that it holds the system fixed while varying
the signal. Table 1 summarises the families and the mechanism that generates each.

The dataset. M100 ExaData [Borghesi et al., 2023] publishes 49.9TB of holistic telemetry
— management, workload, facility and infrastructure — from Marconi100 across two and a
half years. Two of its derived releases matter here. The unsupervised anomaly-detection set
aggregates IPMI metrics into 15-minute periods with average, standard deviation, minimum
and maximum per window, plus a Nagios-derived label; our common analysis grid matches that
choice, though we apply it to all nine families rather than IPMI alone. The thermal-hazard set
reduces sampling from 20 seconds to 10 minutes, and the authors state the trade explicitly: that
rate “will preserve room- and node-level thermal transient but discard computing-component-
level ones”. That is a direct statement of what aggregation costs, and it applies to our results
as much as to theirs (oi-4).
What this telemetry is normally used for. Thedominantapplicationisanomaly detection
rather than forecasting. Borghesi et al. [2019] train per-node autoencoders on HPC monitoring
data and report detecting previously unseen anomalies at accuracies between 88% and 96%.
Detectionaskswhetherthepresentsampleisunusualgivenalearnedmodelofnormalbehaviour;
forecasting asks what the next sample will be. The former can succeed on signals whose future
values are not predictable at all, which is one reason the difficulty we measure has attracted
little attention.
F eature-based characterisation of time series. Our method follows the tradition of de-
scribing a series by a fixed feature vector rather than by a fitted model. Lubba et al. [2019]
reduce 4791 candidate features to 22 by selecting for classification performance and minimal
redundancy across 93 datasets, achieving a roughly 1000-fold reduction in computation for an
average accuracy loss of 7%, and covering linear and non-linear autocorrelation, successive dif-
ferences, value distributions and outliers, and fluctuation scaling. Our profile is smaller and
chosen for interpretability by mechanism rather than by classification performance, but the
premise — that a compact, model-free description captures much of what matters about a
series — is theirs.
Capacity and forecast accuracy . Our finding that heavier models do worse has a direct
precedent outside HPC. Zeng et al. [2023] introduce one-layer linear models and report that they
outperform sophisticated Transformer-based long-term forecasters “in all cases, and often by a
large margin” across nine real-world datasets, arguing that permutation-invariant self-attention
discards temporal ordering. Our result is the same shape in a different domain and at the
opposite end of the complexity range: on this telemetry a persistence forecast beats gradient-
boosted trees and neural networks at every horizon tested. Where they compare a linear model
against Transformers, we find the useful baseline is simpler still.

4 Method
4.1 Campaign census
Tar members are read header-only, so cataloguing all 31 archives costs seconds and no disk.
For each extracted partition we then compute a per-series census: one row per (plugin, metric,
entity, month) recording sample count, observed cadence, cadence jitter, gap fraction, coverage,
missingness, value range, distinct-value count and quantisation step. The census is deliberately
descriptive — it contains no predictability measure and no model — so that a later finding of
between-family differences cannot be an artefact of having looked first.
4.2 The entity panel
Node-level series of one metric are highly redundant — nodes share racks, inlet air, cooling
zones, and workload — and are therefore not independent samples (oi-9). Analysis runs on a
fixed panel of 96 entities whose membership is a stable hash of the entity label under the project
seed. Because the hash sees only the label, panel membership is identical across every metric
and every month, so cross-metric comparisons are made on the same physical machines rather
than on a fresh random slice.
4.3 A-priori signal taxonomy
Labels are assigned from the plugin and metricname alone, before any predictability is mea-
sured, so that a finding of between-family differences cannot be a relabelling of the result it is
meant to explain.
4.4 Model-free predictability measures
Each series receives a profile of measures grouped by the mechanism it probes: entropy and
complexity (permutation entropy, spectral entropy, Lempel–Ziv); periodicity (dominant-period
strength, daily seasonal strength); memory (autocorrelation at lag one and one day, detrended
fluctuation exponent); stationarity (block-mean drift ratio, variance ratio); and shape (jump
fraction, repetition fraction, longest flat run, excess kurtosis of differences). Each returnsnan
rather than raising on degenerate input, because constant series are the common case here rather
than an edge case (§5.2). Implementations are validated against series whose answers are known
by construction — a ramp, a sine, white noise, a random walk — in the accompanying test
suite.
No single measure suffices, and the reason is instructive. Permutation entropy assigns 0.000
both to monotone counters, which have some 17850 distinct values, and to alarm states, which
have two: a monotone series has exactly one ordering pattern and a step function almost
never changes. Both are easy to forecast, for opposite structural reasons, and entropy cannot
distinguish them (oi-11).
4
4.5 Forecast protocol
Skill is measured under rolling-origin evaluation with strict causality asserted per split. Errors
are scaled by MASE with a denominator computed from the training window only; scaling by
test-window statistics is the most common silent leak in forecasting benchmarks and would
compress exactly the between-tier differences this study reads. The seasonal period is fixed per
series a priori from its own cadence, with a non-seasonal robustness check (oi-5).
MASE is undefined where the in-sample seasonal-naive error vanishes. This is not an edge
case here: a substantial share of control-plane series are constant (§5.1), and near-perfectly
periodic windows yield denominators at the level of floating-point noise. Such series are reported
as undefined rather than assigned a spuriously large score.
4.6 Linking analysis
Skill is regressed on the predictability profile, cross-validated by holding out anentire signal
family. A random split would let the model memorise family-specific quirks and report a
flattering R2; a held-out family forces the relation to transfer to signal physics it has never seen,
which is what a portable difficulty law would require. EveryR2 is reported against a train-mean
baseline, so “the measures explain skill” means better than knowing nothing rather than better
than zero. Three ablations exist to attack the headline: dropping the instrument covariates,
keeping only them, and removing the tie-biased entropy. Marginal correlations use a cluster
bootstrap over metrics, because series of one metric across nodes are not independent (oi-9).
5 Results
5.1 The monitoring system is not stationary
Across the 31-month campaign we catalogue 10504 metric partitions comprising 573 distinct
(plugin, metric) pairs. Only 104 — all of them IPMI — are present in every month. Ganglia’s
metric count runs 0, 33, 73, 49, 157, 177 and is not monotonic; SLURM’s runs 20, 2, 0, 2, 36;
Schneider first appears in 21-03 and Vertiv and Weather in 21-04.
Figure 1 shows the pattern. Any claim compared across months therefore confounds signal
behaviour with changes to the instrument, and we restrict cross-month statements accordingly
(oi-2).
5.2 Much of what is monitored never changes
Across 159506 series the fraction constant over a whole month varies by more than a factor of
seven between families: 64.8% for Nagios, 47.0% Schneider, 42.9% Logics and Ganglia, 33.1%
Vertiv, against 10.5% SLURM, 8.4% IPMI and 0% Weather. MASE is undefined for such series
— the seasonal-naive denominator is zero — and they are excluded from skill statistics and
counted rather than silently dropped. Figure 2 gives the breakdown.
5.3 Regularity and interruption are different properties
Sampling irregularity is routinely summarised by the coefficient of variation of the inter-sample
interval. On this data that measure is misleading: it reports IPMI as the least regular family,
contradicting the dataset documentation. IPMI in fact haszeroseries with a jittery interval and
100% with a gap exceeding a hundred times the median — its sampling is exact and universally
interrupted, and the rare enormous gaps dominate the coefficient of variation. Ganglia is the
only family whose interval genuinely wanders (81% of series), which is what the documentation
reports. We therefore measure jitter and interruption separately (oi-1); Figure 3 shows that
the two are nearly orthogonal.

5.4 Forecast skill and model capacity
Figure 4 shows median skill by tier and horizon on 1082 series, pooling every model within a
tier. Capacity does not buy skill: the naive tier leads at every horizon and the best single model
throughout is persistence.
Pooling penalises a tier for containing a deliberately weak member, so we also compare each
tier’s best model per series, paired across series. That comparison is both more favourable to
the sophisticated tiers and more conclusive against them. At a one-hour horizon the per-series
best is 0.945 (IQR[0.70, 1.00]) for naive, 0.861 for statistical, 0.747 for boosted trees and 0.643
for neural networks; a paired Wilcoxon test of naive against each other tier rejects equality at
every horizon and by a wide margin (p < 10−20 against every tier at one hour, andp < 10−90
against the neural tier). The ordering is not an artefact of pooling. At a 24-hour horizon
even the best naive model is negative (-0.284), so the claim that no tier beats a same-step
naive forecast holds at a day but not at three hours, where the per-series best naive model
still scores 0.880. One qualification remains: the ordering depends on the baseline. Against a
daily-seasonal denominator the statistical tier leads instead, and boosted trees overtake naive
at the shortest horizon (oi-5).
5.5 Control: is this an artefact of recursive forecasting?
Every learned model above forecasts recursively — predict a step, append it, predict again
— so error compounds, and the collapse of the learned tiers at long horizons might be that
compounding rather than anything about the signals. This is the most plausible alternative
explanation for the result, so we test it rather than caveat it. We fit the same gradient-boosted
learner two ways on the same series, origins and horizons: recursively, anddirectly, trained to
map a lag window onto the valueh steps ahead in a single prediction.
Recursion does cost a great deal — forecasting directly recovers between 0.07 and 0.85 of
skill depending on horizon, most at one and three hours — so the caveat was warranted. But
the conclusion is unaffected: the direct model still loses to persistence at every horizon tested
(Table 2). The capacity result is a property of the problem, not of the recursion.

5.6 Predictability does not transfer across families
Individuallythemeasures correlatewith skill. Cluster-robustover261metricclusters, repetition
fraction reaches r = +0 .37 (95% CI [0.26, 0.49]) and permutation entropy −0.33 (95% CI
[−0.44, −0.22]).
Jointly they generalise only weakly. Holding out each of 15 families in turn gives amedian
out-of-sample R2 of +0.06 for a random forest, positive for 9 of the 15, and ranging from+0.25
(scheduler state) to−1.88 (monotone error counters). The mean over families,−0.16, is pulled
below zero by the few negative cases; excluding the worst it is−0.04. Reporting the mean alone
would describe the tail rather than the relation.
The failure is therefore specific rather than general, and its cause is identifiable. Mono-
tone counters are forecast almost exactly by a drift model, because they increase steadily by
construction; nothing in a predictability profile built from entropy, periodicity, memory and
stationarity anticipates that a signal will be trivial for one particular extrapolator. A profile
that included a monotonicity or unit-root term would likely absorb the case, which is a concrete
direction rather than a general indictment. What the evidence supports is that the measures
transfer weakly and inconsistently, not that no portable notion of difficulty exists.
The result is not an artefact of instrumentation. Removing the sampling and quantisation
covariates slightlyimproves the model (−0.14 against −0.16), and using them alone collapses
it (−15.0). Whatever the measures capture, it is not how the data was sampled — which is the

7 Conclusion
We set out to show that the difficulty of forecasting HPC telemetry is an intrinsic, measurable
property of the signals. Half of that survives. Difficulty is not a modelling deficiency: across
four tiers spanning naive baselines to neural networks, more capacity produced less skill, and
beyond three hours nothing beat repeating the last value.

The other half does not, though less completely than a single summary statistic suggests.
Measures that correlate with skill within the pooled data transfer to an unseen family only
weakly: a median held-outR2 of +0.06, positive for 9 of 15 families and negative for the rest.
Forecast difficulty here is closer to a family-specific property than a portable law, and the
measure carrying most of the signal — how often a series holds still — is nearer a restatement
than an explanation.
The one clean failure is instructive. Monotone counters defeat the model entirely because a
drift forecaster handles them almost exactly, and no measure of entropy, periodicity, memory
or stationarity predicts that a signal will be trivial for one specific extrapolator. That suggests
the profile is missing a term rather than that the programme is misconceived, and it is a cheap
thing for future work to test.
For operators the practical reading is narrower than we hoped but still useful: on this data,
at these horizons, a persistence forecast is a strong default — significantly better than every
heavier tier we tested, at every horizon — and the burden of proof lies with any claim that a
larger model improves on it.
